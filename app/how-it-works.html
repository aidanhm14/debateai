What actually happens, in five steps

  1. You get a topic. Something people actually disagree about, chosen because a strong case exists on both sides. You do not have to think of one.
  2. You pick a side. You do not have to agree with it. Arguing the side you dislike is the useful part.
  3. You talk for a few minutes. Out loud, on a clock.
  4. Someone argues back. A real person if one is around, an AI if not. They interrupt. You answer.
  5. A judge says who won, and why. In writing, in a few seconds, with the reasons laid out.

That is one round. About twenty minutes, four turns, prep included. You do not need to know anything about debating to start, and nobody has to watch.

Five words you will see

Question
The thing being argued. It can be something everyday, like "should cities ban private cars from their centres?"
Side
Which half you argue. Pro supports the claim. Con opposes it. You can choose or be assigned one.
Response
Your answer to the other person's point. A strong response deals with their reasoning instead of changing the subject.
Score
A score out of 100 for how well you argued, separate from who won the round. You can lose and still score well.
Ballot
What the judge writes at the end: the decision, the reasons for it, your score out of 100, and what to fix next time.

The round itself

Every round uses one casual 1v1 structure. Pro opens, Con answers and builds a case, then both sides get a closing turn. The clock keeps things moving without asking anyone to learn tournament procedure.

The live show keeps the setup casual. One person argues Pro, one argues Con, and both speak on the same clock. There is no tournament rulebook to learn before starting.

In a live round with an audience, a fact checker reads the speech as it is delivered and raises a card to everyone watching when a number is checkably, badly wrong. It never scores, it never says who is winning, and the ballot never reads it. A card only goes up when a second pass, whose job is to refute the first, fails to knock it down.

The judge

The AI judge tracks each claim, what answered it, and what went unanswered. The ballot states a decision with reasoning, scores each person out of 100, names the issue that decided it, and lists fixes for both sides. It decides on the argument in front of it, not on the side it likes.

An AI judge owned by the people running the contest is worth nothing unless you can check it, so the method is published rather than described. The criteria are versioned and hashed before the round, and a ballot stays attributable to the criteria that judged it.

How it works FAQ

I have never debated. Is this for me?

Yes. Every round is a casual 1v1 with no format jargon, prepared case, or tournament rulebook. You make a point, say why it holds, and answer what the other person said. The judge writes its reasoning in plain English, so a round you lose still tells you what to fix.

How does Debatable work?

Pick an opponent, a real person from the live queue or an AI that argues out loud, take a side on a question, and argue a timed round. When it ends, an AI judge writes a full ballot: who won, why, a score out of 100 for each side, and what to fix.

Do I debate a person or an AI?

Your choice. The live queue pairs you with a real person on video, and neither side enters the room until both confirm they are there. The voice round puts you against an AI opponent that speaks and responds to your argument. AI opponents are always labeled as AI.

Can I argue on the live stream?

Yes, if a host takes your hand. While a debate is broadcasting on the watch hub, the player carries a Join button: you say whether you want to take turns or argue on an open floor, and the host picks from the raised hands. A stage round is 18+ and recorded, it gets a full ballot from the same judge panel, and it does not move your rating, because the host chose who came on.

What is the difference between taking turns and an open floor?

Taking turns is the ordinary round: one person talks at a time, on a clock. An open floor leaves both microphones live for one stretch and cutting in is allowed. The judge scores an open floor by a method written for a conversation: interrupting never wins or loses a point, talking more is not arguing better, and a point that got cut off and was never finished counts for neither side. Dodging a direct question counts against you the same way an unanswered claim does.

What if nobody else is online?

You can keep waiting in the live queue or choose the clearly labeled AI opponent after a short wait. The voice round is available on demand at any hour. Clash Hour runs every day in three sessions: 12:00 AM, 3:00 PM and 7:00 PM Eastern.

How does the AI judge decide who won?

It tracks each claim, what answered it, and what went unanswered. The ballot states a decision with reasoning, gives each person a score out of 100, and lists what both sides should fix. It judges the argument in front of it, not the side it prefers.

Can I check the judge, or appeal a decision?

Yes. The rubric is published and versioned before the round, ranked rounds go to a panel of three different model families rather than one call, an even split records as unresolved instead of being tie-broken, and either debater can appeal within 72 hours to a person who was not in the round. Every ballot records which models judged it. Details are on the judge integrity page.

Which debate format does it use?

Debatable uses one casual 1v1 structure. One person argues for, one argues against, and both get a fair chance to open, respond, and close. Competitive debate formats are not part of the public experience.

How long does a round take?

A round is four short turns and takes about twenty minutes including prep. The ballot lands in seconds.

Do I need a microphone or a camera?

A microphone for any spoken round, and the browser asks for it when you start. Camera is only for live human rounds on video, and you can turn it off there. If you would rather not speak at all, the typed surface runs the same casual 1v1 in text.

Do I need an account to start?

For an AI round, no: you can finish one before signing in. Live video requires a Google or verified phone sign-in before you enter the queue, so another person is not paired with an unaccountable opponent. Signing in saves rounds, ballots, and your history across devices.

What does it cost?

A Free plan is available. Paid tiers and current limits are listed on the pricing page.

Can other people watch my round?

Live rounds are public, so anyone can spectate one that is running. That is deliberate: rounds people can watch are rounds people can learn from.

What happens after the ballot?

Signed-in rounds save to your history and build your style profile. Ranked rounds move you on the public leaderboard, and a judged voice assessment can become a free certificate with a public verify URL. Completed public rounds get their own page with the transcript and ballot on it.

Will a coach think this counts as cheating?

It is practice, not a submission. You give the speeches yourself, on a clock, and the ballot tells you what to fix. Nothing here writes a case you then hand in as your own work. If your league has a rule about AI in prep, follow the league.
